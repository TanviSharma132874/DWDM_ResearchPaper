PAPER 16 | META-ANALYSIS
Association between air pollution and allergic upper respiratory diseases: a meta-analysis
Zhi-Qiang Zhang, J.-Y. Li, Q. Guo et al.
European Respiratory Review, 2025; 34(176): 240266
Links: DOI | Consensus summary
## 1. Background and objective
Allergic rhinitis, asthma and chronic sinusitis are common upper and lower airway diseases that reduce quality of life.
Earlier studies disagreed on how strongly air pollution is involved, so this paper combines them statistically.
Objective: estimate prevalence and risk of allergic upper respiratory diseases linked to air pollution, and see who is most affected.
## 2. Dataset used
No new patients were recruited. The data are results extracted from published epidemiological studies.

## 3. Methodology flowchart

Figure 1. Meta-analysis workflow
Step by step
Collect published studies on air pollution and allergic upper airway disease.
Extract prevalence and odds ratios, plus pollutant, region, sex and age.
Pool results with random-effects models because studies differ widely.
Measure inconsistency between studies (I²) and run subgroup analyses.
Test for publication bias (Egger and Begg).

## 5. What it means
Air pollution is positively correlated with allergic rhinitis and asthma, raising the risk of allergic upper respiratory disease.
Effects were larger for men, young people and people in high-pollution urban areas of Europe, which suggests prevention should target those groups.
## 6. Limitations to mention
Heterogeneity is extreme (I² about 99.9% for the prevalence estimates), so the pooled numbers are rough averages across very different populations.
Egger test for allergic rhinitis prevalence was p = 0.035, which hints at possible publication bias.
Only 4 studies informed the CRS estimate, so the CRS result is the least certain.
Observational data show association, not proof of cause.

## 8. Likely questions and short answers
Q: What does a pooled prevalence of 16% mean?
A: A weighted average of prevalence across the 15 studies; it is not the rate in any single country.
Q: Why is heterogeneity so high?
A: The studies differ in country, age group, pollutant and design, so I² is near 100%.
Q: Does this prove pollution causes CRS?
A: No. It shows association; causal work needs designs such as cohorts or Mendelian randomisation.
## 9. How it fits the team project
Gives the big-picture evidence that pollution matters, before the CRS-specific papers (17–19).
